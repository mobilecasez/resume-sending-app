How Long Should a Resume Be? One Page vs Two, by Country

"Keep it to one page" is the most repeated resume rule there is, and it's only right some of the time. Here's how to pick the right length for your experience and the country you're applying in — and what to cut when it runs long.

By The CVApplyr Team·8 min read·Updated October 2026

You've finished your resume and it spills four lines onto page two. So you shrink the font, nudge the margins, delete a job you were proud of, and it still doesn't fit. Or the opposite: you've got two years of experience and a page that looks half empty.

Resume length causes a surprising amount of stress for something that, in the end, is mostly about one question: does every line help this reader decide to call you? The page count follows from the answer, not the other way round.

Still, there are real conventions, and they differ more than most advice admits. A recruiter in New York, one in London and one in Munich will each have a different idea of "normal". Here's the short answer, then the reasoning.

The short answer

  • Students, new graduates and early-career (under ~5 years): one page, almost everywhere.
  • Mid-career (roughly 5–15 years): one page is common in the US; two pages is normal in the UK, Ireland, Australia and most of Europe.
  • Senior, technical or highly specialised: two pages is standard nearly everywhere. Three is rarely needed outside academia.
  • Academic, research and medical CVs: a different document entirely. Publications, grants and teaching are listed in full, so length follows the record.

If you're unsure, aim for the shorter option and make sure your strongest, most relevant material lands near the top of page one. That's where the decision is actually made.

Why page count matters less than the top third

The one-page rule exists for a good reason, just not the one people think. It isn't that recruiters refuse to turn the page. It's that they decide very quickly whether to keep reading at all. In the well-known Ladders eye-tracking study (2018), recruiters spent around 6–7 seconds on their first pass over a resume. And a typical corporate opening attracts around 250 applications (Glassdoor), so that quick pass is how the pile gets sorted.

Seven seconds is enough to read your name, your most recent title and employer, a summary line or two, and maybe the first few bullet points. That's it. So the useful question isn't "one page or two?" but "what does someone see in the first seven seconds, and does it make them want the rest?" A sharp two-page resume beats a cramped one-pager every time. A padded two-pager loses to a focused single page every time.

Why getting the length right is genuinely hard

  • The rules conflict. US advice says one page; UK advice says two; German applications expect a tabular CV, often with a photo. Most people read all three and end up confused.
  • Everything feels relevant to you. You remember the work behind every line. The reader doesn't, and can't tell which bullet mattered unless you show them.
  • One resume, many jobs. A version that fits one role perfectly is padded for another. The "right" length shifts with every application.
  • Formatting tricks backfire. Tiny fonts, narrow margins and text boxes squeeze things onto a page, but they hurt readability and can confuse the applicant tracking systems a large majority of mid-size and large employers use to parse applications (Jobscan and industry surveys).

Length by country: what's normal where

United States and Canada

One page is the default for anyone under about ten years of experience, and still common beyond that. Two pages is accepted for senior and technical roles. No photo, no date of birth, no marital status — including them can actually work against you.

United Kingdom, Ireland, Australia and New Zealand

It's usually called a CV, and two pages is the norm for experienced candidates. One page is fine for graduates. Again, no photo or personal details beyond contact information.

Germany, Austria and Switzerland

The Lebenslauf is typically one to two pages, laid out as a clean, dated, reverse-chronological table. A professional photo is still common, though no longer required. Precise dates (month and year) matter more here than almost anywhere, so gaps are noticed.

India

Two pages is common, and early-career candidates often list academic and internship projects in detail, because employers look for evidence of hands-on work. Detail is welcome; repetition isn't.

Elsewhere in Europe and beyond

Many countries sit between these poles, and EU applicants sometimes meet the Europass format. When in doubt, look at how the employer's own job posting is written: long, formal postings tend to welcome fuller CVs; short, punchy ones signal a preference for brevity.

Applying abroad? Match the convention of the country you're applying to, not the one you're from. A one-page US-style resume can look thin in Germany; a two-page CV with a photo can look out of place in the US.

What to cut first when it runs long

If you're over, don't touch the font size yet. Cut in this order:

  1. Old roles. Anything more than 10–15 years back can shrink to a single line (title, employer, dates) or go into a short "Earlier career" section.
  2. Duty-only bullets. "Responsible for managing the team" says nothing a title doesn't. Keep bullets that show a result, a scale or a specific skill the job asks for.
  3. Duplicate bullets. If three jobs each say you "collaborated with stakeholders", say it once, in the role where it mattered most.
  4. Generic skills. "Microsoft Office", "communication", "hard-working". Replace them with the specific tools and skills from the job description.
  5. "References available on request" and objective statements. Both are assumed or outdated. A two-line summary tailored to the role does more work.

What to add when it's too short

Half a page of white space can look like a lack of effort. If you're short, don't pad — add evidence:

  • Projects. University, personal or freelance projects with a clear outcome are real experience, especially for new graduates and career changers.
  • Numbers. Turn "helped improve onboarding" into "rewrote the onboarding guide, cutting new-starter questions roughly in half". Specifics take up space honestly.
  • Relevant coursework, certifications and tools. Only the ones that match the job.
  • Volunteering and leadership. Running a society, organising an event or mentoring counts when you show what you did.

The real fix: one length per job, not one resume forever

Here's the part most length advice skips. The ideal resume for a backend engineering role and the ideal resume for a technical product role, from the same person, contain different bullets — and so they can be different lengths. Tailoring a resume to the job description measurably lifts callback rates (Jobscan), and cutting what isn't relevant to this job is the easiest tailoring there is. The problem is time: doing it by hand for every application takes far longer than anyone has.

Frequently asked questions

Is a two-page resume bad?

No. A two-page resume is normal for experienced candidates and is the standard CV length in the UK, Ireland, Australia and much of Europe. What hurts is a second page filled with old or irrelevant detail. If both pages earn their place and your strongest material is on page one, two pages is fine.

Should a student or new graduate have a one-page resume?

In most markets, yes. With limited work history, one page forces you to lead with the education, projects, internships and skills that match the job. Stretching thin experience onto two pages usually makes it look weaker, not stronger.

Does an ATS care how long my resume is?

An applicant tracking system parses the text regardless of page count, so length itself rarely gets you rejected. What matters to the ATS is clean formatting it can read and the skills and keywords from the job description. Length matters to the human who reads it afterwards.

Can I shrink the font and margins to fit one page?

Only a little. Body text below about 10 points and margins under about half an inch make a resume harder to read on screen and in print. If it only fits by squeezing, cut content instead: older roles, duplicate bullets and generic skills are the usual candidates.
