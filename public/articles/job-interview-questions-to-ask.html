Questions to Ask in a Job Interview: 21 That Actually Impress

"Do you have any questions for us?" sounds like a polite wrap-up. It isn't. It's the one part of the interview where you set the agenda. Here are 21 questions worth asking, who to ask them to, the ones to avoid, and a 15-minute way to prepare them.

By The CVApplyr Team·9 min read·Updated October 2026

You've spent forty minutes answering questions. Your mouth is dry, you've just finished a story about a project that went sideways, and the interviewer leans back: "So, do you have any questions for us?"

Most candidates treat this as the cool-down lap. They ask something generic, or say "No, I think you've covered everything," and the interview ends on a flat note. But interviewers notice what you ask. Good questions show you've done your homework, that you're thinking about the actual work, and that you're choosing them as much as they're choosing you.

Why your questions matter more than you think

By the time you're in an interview, you've already beaten long odds. A typical corporate opening attracts around 250 applicants (Glassdoor), and a recruiter's first pass over each resume takes about six to seven seconds (Ladders). Getting the interview means you survived that. Now you're one of a small group, and the people deciding are looking for reasons to pick one person over the rest.

Your questions do three jobs at once:

  • They prove preparation. A question that references the company's latest product, a recent hire or a line in the job description shows you didn't walk in cold.
  • They show how you think. Asking how success is measured, or what's blocking the team, signals that you're already thinking like someone in the role.
  • They help you decide. An interview is a two-way evaluation. The answers tell you whether you actually want this job, which is information you can't get from a careers page.

Why it's harder than it looks

If good questions are so useful, why do so many candidates fumble them?

  • Your prepared questions get answered. You arrive with "What does a typical day look like?" and the interviewer explains exactly that in minute ten. Now you have nothing.
  • You're tired. After 40 minutes of performing, your brain wants the interview to end, not to start a new thread.
  • You don't know who you're talking to. A question that's perfect for the hiring manager lands oddly with a recruiter, and vice versa.
  • You're preparing for five interviews at once. When you're applying widely, deep research for every company feels impossible, so the questions end up generic.

The fix is to prepare more questions than you need, group them by who you're meeting, and anchor at least one to something specific about the company.

Questions for the recruiter (phone or video screen)

Recruiters know the process, the timeline and the practicalities. This is the right place for logistics, including pay.

  1. "What does the rest of the interview process look like, and roughly how long does it take?" Practical, and it tells you when to follow up.
  2. "What's the salary range for this role?" Perfectly normal with a recruiter, and it saves everyone time.
  3. "Why is this position open?" Growth, a promotion or a departure each tell you something different.
  4. "What do the people who do well on this team have in common?" Recruiters hear this from hiring managers all the time.
  5. "Is there anything in my background you'd want me to explain more to the hiring manager?" Lets you fix a weak spot before the next round.

Questions for the hiring manager

This is the person you'd report to. Focus on the work, the expectations and how they lead.

  1. "What would a great first 90 days look like in this role?" The single most useful question you can ask. It reveals priorities and expectations at once.
  2. "How will you measure success for this person after a year?" Shows you care about outcomes, not just tasks.
  3. "What's the biggest challenge the team is facing right now?" Then listen carefully, because it's your cue to mention relevant experience.
  4. "What's something the last person in this role did really well, or that you'd like done differently?" Honest answers here are gold.
  5. "How do you prefer to give feedback?" You'll learn a lot about working with them day to day.
  6. "I saw that [recent launch / expansion / news]. How does that affect this team?" Your research question. Fill in a real detail.
  7. "What does the team's week usually look like: meetings, deep work, collaboration?" Better than "what's a typical day", because it gets concrete.
Tip: Write your research question last, the night before. A detail from the company's news or the job description is what makes a question sound like yours instead of something from a list.

Questions for the team or a peer interviewer

  1. "What do you enjoy most about working here?" Watch for a genuine answer versus a pause.
  2. "How do you and the rest of the team work together on a typical project?"
  3. "What tools or processes would I be using every day?"
  4. "What's one thing you wish you'd known when you joined?" People love answering this one.

Questions for the final round (senior leaders)

  1. "Where do you see this team or product in two years?" Leaders like talking strategy, and it shows you're thinking long term.
  2. "What would make you confident this hire was the right decision?"
  3. "How has the company changed since you joined?"
  4. "Is there anything about my background that makes you hesitate?" Bold, but it gives you one last chance to address a concern in the room instead of leaving it for the debrief.
  5. "What are the next steps, and when should I expect to hear back?" Always end here. It sets the clock for your follow-up.

Questions to avoid

  • Anything a two-minute search would answer. "So, what does the company do?" signals zero preparation.
  • Perks first. Leading with holidays, remote days or "how soon can I get promoted?" in a first conversation with the hiring manager makes it sound like the job is secondary. Save these for the recruiter or the offer stage.
  • "Did I get the job?" or "How did I do?" It puts the interviewer on the spot. Ask about next steps instead.
  • Yes/no questions. "Do you like working here?" gets a one-word answer. "What do you enjoy most about working here?" gets a conversation.
  • Gossip. Questions about rumours, layoffs you read about on a forum, or a former employee's complaint rarely land well. If something genuinely worries you, ask neutrally: "How has the team handled the changes this year?"

How to prepare your questions in 15 minutes

  1. Re-read the job description (3 min). Circle the two responsibilities that seem most important. One of your questions should be about each.
  2. Check who you're meeting (3 min). Look up their role. A recruiter, a future manager and a VP each get different questions.
  3. Find one recent detail (5 min). A product launch, a new office, a funding round, a talk the manager gave. Turn it into question 11.
  4. Pick six, write them down (4 min). Two about the work, two about the team, one about the future, one about next steps. It's fine to glance at your notes; it looks prepared, not nervous.

If your prepared questions get answered during the interview, don't panic. Say so: "You actually covered most of what I wanted to ask, especially about the first 90 days. Can I follow up on the migration project you mentioned?" A follow-up on something they said is often the best question of all, because it proves you were listening.

After the interview

The answers to your questions are also your raw material for the next step. The detail the manager shared about their biggest challenge is the perfect line for a short thank-you email the same day. And the timeline the recruiter gave you tells you exactly when a polite check-in is appropriate, and when it would be too soon.

Interviews will always be a little nerve-racking. But "Do you have any questions for us?" doesn't have to be the awkward bit at the end. Prepare six, ask two or three, make one of them specific, and finish by asking about next steps. It's the easiest way to leave the room as the candidate they remember.

Frequently asked questions

How many questions should I ask at the end of an interview?

Prepare five or six and expect to ask two or three. Some will get answered during the conversation, and time often runs short. Asking two thoughtful questions is far better than rushing through a list of six.

Is it bad to say I have no questions at the end of an interview?

It usually reads as low interest or low preparation, even if neither is true. If your prepared questions were all answered, say so and ask a follow-up on something the interviewer mentioned, or ask what the next steps are.

Can I ask about salary in a job interview?

With the recruiter, yes: asking about the salary range for the role is normal and saves everyone time. With the hiring manager in an early round, it is usually better to focus on the work and leave compensation details to the recruiter or a later stage.

What is a good final question to ask an interviewer?

A strong closing question is: Is there anything about my background that makes you hesitate? It shows confidence and gives you a chance to address a concern before the decision is made. Follow it with a simple question about next steps and timing.
