What to Put for Salary Expectations on a Job Application

It's one small box near the end of the form, and it can undo everything above it. Here's how to fill it in so it keeps you in the running — without quietly giving away a pay rise.

By The CVApplyr Team·9 min read·Updated October 2026

You've uploaded the resume, retyped your work history into the boxes, written a few lines about why you want the role. Then the form asks: "What are your salary expectations?" And there's a little red asterisk next to it.

Type too high and you worry you'll be binned before anyone reads a word. Type too low and you might have just negotiated against yourself, months before an offer exists. So people freeze, guess, or type "0" and hope for the best.

There's a calmer way to think about it. The salary box on an application isn't a negotiation. It's a filter. Once you understand what it's filtering for, the right answer gets much easier to find.

Why employers ask before they've even met you

From your side, the question feels premature. From the employer's side, it's practical. A typical corporate opening attracts around 250 applicants (Glassdoor), and the team hiring has a budget for the role that is usually set before the job is posted. Asking about expectations early is a cheap way to avoid spending three interview rounds on someone who, it turns out, needs 40% more than they can pay.

That's why the question often sits inside the applicant tracking system as a screening field, alongside things like work authorisation and notice period. A large majority of mid-size and large employers use an ATS (Jobscan and other industry surveys), and many let recruiters sort or filter on these answers. Your number may be looked at long before your experience is.

So the job of your answer is simple to state: stay inside the band the employer can afford, without dropping below what you're worth. Everything else in this article is about finding that band and expressing it well.

Why this little box is so hard

  • You don't know their budget. Unless the posting lists a range, you're guessing at a number someone else has already decided.
  • Job titles lie. A "Senior Analyst" at a 30-person startup and a "Senior Analyst" at a bank can be paid very differently, and the same title means different things in Berlin, Bangalore and Boston.
  • Anchoring works against you. Whatever you type early tends to become the reference point later. Lowball now and a "generous" offer may still sit below the role's real range.
  • Forms are rigid. Some fields take only digits. Some want annual, some monthly, some hourly. Some force a currency you don't think in.
  • You answer it fifty times. In an active search the question comes up on almost every portal, and it's tempting to stop thinking and type whatever you typed last time — even when the role, level or country is different.

Step 1: Find the real range before you type anything

Ten minutes of research turns a guess into a defensible number. Look for, in roughly this order:

  1. The posting itself. Read to the bottom. A growing number of places require employers to publish pay ranges — several US states and cities (including Colorado, California, Washington and New York City) have pay-transparency laws, and the EU Pay Transparency Directive requires employers to share the starting pay or range before the interview as member states bring it into national law. If a range is listed, that's your best data point by far.
  2. Similar roles at the same company. Other postings from the same employer often reveal its bands, even if this one doesn't.
  3. Similar roles nearby. Three to five comparable postings in the same city and seniority, with published ranges, give you a credible picture of the local market.
  4. Salary sites and people. Aggregated salary data is a useful cross-check; a friend in the industry or a recruiter is often better. Ask what the role pays, not what they earn.

From that, write down three numbers for yourself: your walk-away (the lowest you'd genuinely accept), your target, and a stretch number at the top of what the market seems to support. You won't type all three, but knowing them makes every version of the question easy.

Think in total package, not just base. Bonus, equity, pension contributions, remote days and holiday allowance all have real value. If the base is a little lower than you hoped but the package is strong, your walk-away number may move — decide that before the form asks, not under pressure later.

Step 2: Match your answer to the field in front of you

If the field accepts text

Give a range, and make the bottom a number you'd actually accept — employers reasonably assume they can offer the low end. Keep it fairly tight (around 10–15% wide) so it reads as considered rather than "anything, please". Something like: "£52,000–£58,000 base, flexible depending on the overall package." If the posting lists a range, sit inside it: mid-range if you meet the requirements, upper half if you exceed them.

If the field accepts only a number

Enter a single figure from the upper-middle of your researched range — close to your target, not your walk-away. Avoid placeholders like 0, 1 or 999999. They can trip filters, and a human who sees them later may read them as carelessness. Check whether the form wants annual, monthly or hourly pay, and which currency, before you type.

If the field is optional

You can leave it blank or write "negotiable", which keeps your options open until you know more about the role. This is a reasonable choice when you have little data. If you do have good data and you know your number fits, filling it in can help recruiters prioritise you.

If they ask for your current or past salary

That's a different question from your expectations, and you don't have to treat it as the same thing. Many US jurisdictions restrict employers from asking about pay history, and the EU directive bans the question outright as countries implement it. Where the question is optional or not allowed, simply answer with what you're looking for: "I'm targeting £52,000–£58,000 for this role." Your expectations should be based on the job and the market, not on what a previous employer happened to pay you.

Step 3: Use the cover letter or note to add context

A number in a box has no tone. If your expectation is near the top of a listed range, a single sentence elsewhere can explain why without sounding defensive — for example, a specialist skill the posting asks for, or experience leading the exact kind of project they describe. Keep salary talk out of the letter itself unless the posting explicitly asks for it there; let the letter make the case for the value, and let the number follow.

The same goes for a short note to the recruiter or hiring manager after you apply. You don't need to mention salary at all. A clear, relevant message makes it more likely that a person reads your application properly, rather than judging it on one screening field. Studies suggest referred and personally introduced candidates are hired at far higher rates than cold applicants, and a real conversation is where pay is genuinely negotiated.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Typing your current salary as your expectation. Changing jobs is usually your best opportunity for a raise. Don't give it away in a form field.
  • Copy-pasting the same number everywhere. A role in a different city, at a different level or in a different country deserves its own number.
  • Wide, vague ranges. "£40k–£80k" tells the employer you don't know the market, and invites an offer at the bottom.
  • Mixing up units. Monthly versus annual, gross versus net, local currency versus your home currency. A wrong unit can make a sensible expectation look absurd.
  • Agonising for an hour. Once you've done the research, the answer takes thirty seconds. The research is the work; the box is just formatting.

Frequently asked questions

Should I put a range or a single number for salary expectations?

If the field accepts text, a range is usually best, as long as its bottom is a number you'd genuinely accept. Keep it fairly tight, roughly 10–15% wide, and anchor it on research for the role, level and location. If the field accepts only a number, enter a figure from the upper-middle of your researched range rather than your absolute minimum.

Can I write "negotiable" or leave the salary field blank?

If the field is optional, leaving it blank or writing "negotiable" is fine and keeps your options open. If it's required, a blank or a placeholder like 0 or 1 can get your application filtered out or look careless. Give a researched number or range instead, and add "flexible depending on the full package" where there's space.

What if the job posting already lists a salary range?

Then use it. If the range fits you, give an expectation inside it — typically the middle or upper half, depending on how closely you match the requirements. Asking for far more than a published range signals a mismatch, and asking for less than its bottom leaves money on the table for no benefit.

Do I have to tell employers my current or previous salary?

Expectations and history are different questions, and you can share what you're looking for without disclosing what you earn now. Many US states and cities restrict employers from asking about pay history, and the EU Pay Transparency Directive bans the question as member states bring it into national law. Where it's optional, simply answer with your expectations.
