Can You Reapply to the Same Company? When and How to Try Again

You got the polite rejection email, or just silence. Now a new role has opened at the same company and you're wondering whether trying again is smart or a little embarrassing. In most cases it's smart. Here's when to do it, how long to wait, and what to change.

By The CVApplyr Team·8 min read·Updated October 2026

Some companies stick with you. Maybe it's the product you use every day, the team you'd love to learn from, or simply the best employer in your city. You applied, you waited, and the answer was no. Or there was no answer at all.

The instinct is to cross them off and move on. That's often the wrong call. A rejection usually means "not this application, for this role, right now", not "never". People get hired on a second or third attempt more often than you'd think. The key is that the next attempt has to be different, not just repeated.

The short answer: yes, usually

Reapplying to the same company is normal and generally fine. Recruiters see repeat applicants every week, and most applicant tracking systems are built to handle them. Here's the rule of thumb:

  • A different role that fits you: apply whenever it opens. There's no waiting period worth worrying about.
  • The same role, reposted: reapply if you can show something new, or if you suspect your first application never reached a person.
  • The same or a very similar role after an interview rejection: wait around three to six months and come back measurably stronger.
  • The company states a formal cooling-off period: follow it. Some employers mention one in their careers FAQ or rejection email.

What you want to avoid is the opposite pattern: firing the same resume at five openings at once, or reapplying to the role that rejected you a week ago. That doesn't show persistence. It shows you didn't read the job description.

Why a "no" is rarely about you

It helps to remember the arithmetic. A typical corporate opening attracts around 250 applications (Glassdoor), and only a handful of those people are interviewed. Most rejections happen in the first, fastest pass: recruiters spend around 6–7 seconds on an initial resume scan (Ladders eye-tracking study, 2018), and a large majority of mid-size and large employers run applications through an ATS before a human sees them at all.

So when you're rejected at the application stage, the honest conclusion is usually "my application didn't stand out in a few seconds against a few hundred others", not "this company doesn't want me". Those are very different problems, and the first one is fixable.

Even an interview rejection is often about timing or a narrow comparison: an internal candidate, a budget change, someone with one specific skill you didn't have yet. Hiring managers frequently remember the strong runner-up, and that person is exactly who they'd like to see again for the next opening.

Why reapplying feels so hard

  • It feels like rejection twice. Putting yourself back in front of the same people takes nerve, especially if you liked the interviewers.
  • You don't know what went wrong. Most rejections arrive without feedback, so it's tempting to resend the same thing and hope for a different outcome.
  • You worry they'll remember. They might. Applicant tracking systems usually keep old applications on file. But that works in your favour when the new one is clearly better.
  • You lose track. Months pass, the company posts new roles, and you only notice after they've closed. Or you can't remember which version of your resume you sent last time.

How long to wait before reapplying

Different role: no need to wait

If the new opening is a genuinely different job (a different team, level or focus) that suits your background, apply as soon as you see it. Every role has its own hiring manager and its own pile. Your earlier application for something else barely matters.

Same role, no reply at all

Silence is not a verdict. Your first application may have been filtered by the ATS for missing keywords, arrived after the shortlist was already full, or simply been buried. If the role is reposted, or a near-identical one opens, apply again with a version tailored much more closely to the job description, and this time try to get a person to see it as well.

Same role, rejected after an interview

Give it around three to six months, and come back with something new: a certification, a shipped project, a promotion, a skill you were clearly missing last time. If you got any feedback, close that exact gap and say so.

Check the small print first. A few large employers do set a formal waiting period between applications for the same role. If the careers FAQ or the rejection email mentions one, respect it. Applying early won't be counted for you.

What to change the second time

The worst way to reapply is to resend the same document. The recruiter may even see both side by side. Change these things, in this order:

  1. Tailor to this posting. Re-read the job description line by line and mirror its language for the skills you genuinely have. Tailoring a resume to the job description measurably lifts callback rates (Jobscan), and it's the single most likely reason the first try stalled.
  2. Lead with what's new. Put the strongest evidence of growth since last time in the top third of page one: the new result, the new tool, the bigger scope.
  3. Rewrite the cover letter from scratch. Show you know more about the company now: a recent launch, a market they're entering, a problem the team is clearly working on.
  4. Mention the earlier application only if it helps. One confident line ("Since I last applied, I've led…") works if you interviewed and you've grown. If you never heard back, there's nothing to mention.
  5. Reach a person, not just the portal. Referred candidates are hired at much higher rates, studies suggest (Jobvite / iCIMS). Even a short, polite note to the recruiter or hiring manager means your second application gets read by someone, rather than parsed and ranked.

A short note to send if you interviewed before

If you had a good conversation with someone last time, a brief message alongside your new application can make a real difference. Keep it to three or four sentences:

  • Remind them who you are and which role you interviewed for.
  • Say what has changed since: one specific, relevant thing.
  • Mention the new role you've applied for and why it fits.
  • Thank them, and don't ask them to explain the previous decision.

That's it. No apology, no long backstory. The tone you want is "I stayed interested and kept getting better", which is exactly what a hiring manager hopes to hear.

Frequently asked questions

Does reapplying to the same company look desperate?

Not when it's spaced out and the new application is clearly stronger or aimed at a different role. Recruiters see repeat applicants all the time, and many read a thoughtful second application as genuine interest. What looks bad is applying to every opening at once with the same resume, or reapplying for the same role days after a rejection.

How long should I wait before reapplying to the same company?

For a different role, you can apply whenever a good fit opens. For the same or a very similar role, a gap of around three to six months is a sensible default, ideally with something new to show. Some employers state a formal waiting period in their careers FAQ or rejection email; if they do, respect it.

Will the company still have my old application?

Often, yes. Applicant tracking systems usually keep past applications on file, subject to local data-protection rules, so a recruiter may see your history. That's a reason to make the new application visibly better, not a reason to avoid reapplying.

Should I mention that I applied before?

If you've progressed since then, a short, confident line in your cover letter can help, for example noting what you've learned or built since your last application. If you were never interviewed, you don't need to mention it at all. Never apologise for reapplying.
